Grant readiness: the checks before you apply

By Michael FurthOctober 20263 min read

Most grant applications are decided before anyone writes the narrative. The winners were ready before the NOFO came out: registered, and able to carry the costs until reimbursement arrives. Here is what I check first.

1. Registrations and eligibility

Federal awards require an active SAM.gov registration, which is also where your Unique Entity ID comes from. Registration is free but can take weeks, often because the legal name or address doesn’t match the records it is validated against. Registrations expire, so check the date now, not in the week a deadline falls.

Then read the eligibility section of the NOFO, the Notice of Funding Opportunity, and confirm your entity type and location qualify before anyone drafts a narrative. State programs and foundations set their own rules.

2. A project a funder can fund

Funders fund a defined project with measurable results, and the NOFO’s evaluation criteria say how it will be scored. Be able to answer each of these in a paragraph:

  • What problem are you solving, and for whom?
  • What will you do, and on what timeline?
  • What will be measurably different when you finish?
  • Why is your organization the right one to do it?

If the answers are vague, a one-page logic model will expose the missing step.

3. The budget and the cash

Tie a line-item budget to the activities, with a sentence of justification per cost.

Cost share, or match, is the part you pay for. Check the required share, whether in-kind contributions such as staff time count, and how it must be documented. As a rule, federal match can’t come from another federal award unless the program’s statute allows it. Check what the program allows for indirect costs as well.

Then check how the money arrives. Many programs pay on a reimbursement basis: you spend first and claim it back with documentation, so you finance the project for weeks or months. Others advance funds. The question is whether your working capital or a credit line can carry the gap.

A grant you can’t afford to float is a cash-flow problem with a funder’s name on it.

4. Evidence and partners

Collect data that shows need from sources a reviewer can check. Ask partners early and send a draft. A letter of commitment naming what the partner will contribute outweighs a letter of general support, and takes longer to get than anything else here.

5. A document library

Keep current versions of the documents funders request in one folder: leadership bios, financial statements, the procurement policy. Each application then costs days less.

6. A pipeline, not a scramble

Keep a calendar of programs that fit your plan, with each go / no-go call made early enough that a no costs nothing.

Look past grants too. Working with a regional workforce board, I mapped On-the-Job Training reimbursement and Work-Based Learning onto a growing company’s hiring plan; the money followed hires it was making anyway. OJT generally reimburses part of a new hire’s wages during training, and the agreement usually has to be signed before the person starts. Rules vary by board.

7. Capacity to manage the award

An award brings obligations: costs tracked against the approved budget, and post-award reports filed on schedule, backed by records that survive an audit. Funders who have seen you report cleanly tend to fund you again.

When to pass

Not every program you could win is worth winning. Pass when the match would strain cash, or when the project exists only because the grant does. The registration and the documents carry over to the next cycle anyway.
