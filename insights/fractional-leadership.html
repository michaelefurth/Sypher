Fractional leadership before a full-time hire

By Michael FurthOctober 20263 min read

Most growing businesses reach a point where the owner is the bottleneck. Decisions queue up behind one calendar, and the team stays busy without much moving. The reflex is to hire a senior executive. I usually suggest starting with a fractional one, for reasons that have more to do with defining the job than with saving money.

What the role is

A fractional COO or director of strategic operations holds a real seat in your business for a fixed share of the month, often two days a week or four days a month. The difference from consulting is accountability. A consultant hands you a recommendation. A fractional leader is the owner of record for specific outcomes and answers for the numbers in your operating meetings.

I’ve done this as fractional Director of Strategic Operations for a multi-state firm that funds construction and service contractors, building its sales operating system from scratch. That meant segmenting the market by trade and metro and designing the CRM pipeline and playbook the team works from each day. It is work product rather than advice, and somebody has to own it.

When it fits

  • The work needs senior judgment but not a full calendar. If a full-time executive would have idle afternoons, you’d be paying for them.
  • You tried to hire and couldn’t write the job description. Usually the function doesn’t exist yet. Build it first, and the permanent hire inherits a working system and a scorecard.
  • You’re between stages. A new market or a lender’s diligence has an end date, and the role can too.
  • Cash is tight relative to the need. Per hour, a good fractional leader often costs more than an employee. In total it usually costs less, because you aren’t paying for time the role doesn’t need.

When it doesn’t

If the role means running a large team in real time, a part-time leader will be absent at the moments that matter. If the owner isn’t ready to hand over decisions, a fractional leader becomes an expensive note-taker. And if the work already fills a full week, hire for it; spreading a full-time job across a fractional calendar builds a backlog with a monthly invoice attached.

A fractional leader also has other clients, so a 2 a.m. crisis may wait until morning.

Accountability without decision rights is advice with a longer title.

How to set it up

  1. Write down the first 90 days. Three to five objectives with a measure and a date for each, agreed before the start. If you can’t write them, that is the first project.
  2. Put decision rights on paper. List the decisions I make alone and the ones that need your sign-off, with a spending limit. Settle vendor choice and hiring early.
  3. Fix the operating cadence. Name the days I work on your business and the meetings I attend. Then agree how fast I answer in between, because that expectation causes more friction than anything else.
  4. Keep the scorecard short. Four to six KPIs the function controls, reviewed weekly in the same format. Progress shows, and so does the lack of it.
  5. Plan the handover from day one. Each process is written up as an SOP while it’s built. In the contractor-finance work, the handover was the playbook itself: quick-start onboarding, call scripts, weekly scorecards, a seasonal campaign calendar.

The cost to compare

Owners ask whether they can afford a senior leader. The better question is what the gap costs now: projects parked for months, and owner hours spent on work someone else should own. Put rough numbers on both. If the total beats a monthly retainer, the decision is mostly made. Otherwise keep the money and look again in six months.
