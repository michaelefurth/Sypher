AI for small business: workflow first

By Michael FurthOctober 20263 min read

Most small businesses come at AI backwards. They buy a tool, then look for something for it to do. Six months later they pay for several subscriptions and run exactly as before.

Start with where the hours go

The useful opportunities are rarely glamorous. They’re repetitive, text-heavy tasks that eat a team’s week in fifteen-minute pieces. The same few show up in most businesses I work with:

  • Answering the same handful of customer and prospect questions by email
  • Re-keying details from emails and web forms into a spreadsheet or CRM
  • First drafts of proposals and follow-up emails
  • Summarizing calls and long documents into something a person will read
  • Building the same weekly report from the same exports

Before choosing anything, keep a plain one-week log of every task that repeats and the minutes it took. It makes a better roadmap than any vendor’s feature list.

Score each one

For each task, estimate the hours per month, the cost of an error and how standardized it is. High-volume, low-stakes, well-defined work goes first. Work with legal or financial weight can still use AI, with a person reviewing every output; count that review time in the estimate.

When to leave it alone

Some tasks shouldn’t be automated yet. If the process isn’t defined, AI produces the same inconsistency faster. Low-volume work won’t repay the setup and review time. And if an error would reach a customer before anyone sees it, fix the review step first. In those cases the first project is an SOP.

If reviewing the draft takes as long as writing it, nothing was saved.

Check what you already pay for

Google Workspace and Microsoft 365 both include AI features in some plans, and many CRMs have added their own. Depending on your plan, you may already be paying for drafting and meeting summaries nobody uses. Check before buying anything. One workflow connected to the systems you already run beats five separate apps, each holding its own copy of your data.

Put guardrails in place

  1. Decide what data goes where. Read the vendor’s terms on two points: how long your inputs are kept, and whether they’re used to train models. Client records and NDA material stay out of unapproved tools.
  2. Control access. Use company accounts with MFA rather than personal logins, so access ends when someone leaves.
  3. Keep human review on anything that leaves the building. Customer-facing text and anything with financial or legal weight gets a named reviewer.
  4. Write it down. A one-page usage policy and a few saved prompts keep output consistent across people.
  5. Measure hours returned. Time the task for a week before the change and for four weeks after, including the minutes spent reviewing. If the number holds steady, rework the workflow before spending more.

A realistic first project

A first project should be small enough to build and measure in a few weeks. The one I suggest most often is an intake assistant: web-form and inbox inquiries become structured CRM records, with a person approving each one before it saves. The risk is low, the hours are easy to count, and CRM hygiene improves as a side effect. Once it works and the SOP is written, the second project is usually sitting in the time log.
