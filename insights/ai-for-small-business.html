AI for small business: start with the workflow, not the tool

By Michael FurthOctober 20265 min read

Most small businesses approach AI the wrong way round: they pick a tool, then look for something to do with it. The result is a handful of subscriptions, a few impressive demos and very little change in how the business actually runs.

Start with where the hours go

The best AI opportunities in a small business are rarely glamorous. They are the repetitive, high-volume tasks that quietly consume your team’s week:

  • Answering the same customer and prospect questions
  • Re-keying information from emails and forms into spreadsheets or a CRM
  • Drafting proposals, follow-ups and routine documents
  • Summarizing meetings, calls and long documents
  • Building the same weekly report from the same sources

Spend one week noting every task that is repeated, rules-based and text-heavy. That list is your AI roadmap.

Score each opportunity

For every task, estimate three things: hours per month, cost of an error and how standardized it is. High-hour, low-risk, well-defined work is where to begin. High-risk work, such as anything legal, financial or customer-commitment related, can still benefit from AI, but with a person reviewing every output.

The goal isn’t to replace your people. It’s to give a small team the capacity of a much larger one.

Use what you already own

Many businesses already pay for AI capabilities inside their email, document and CRM platforms without using them. Before buying anything new, check what your existing tools can do. Integration beats accumulation: one well-connected workflow is worth more than five disconnected apps.

Put guardrails in place

  1. Decide what data can be used and keep sensitive client information out of tools that aren’t approved for it.
  2. Keep a human in the loop for anything that goes to a customer or carries financial or legal weight.
  3. Write it down. A one-page usage policy and a few saved prompts or templates make results consistent.
  4. Measure the return. Track hours saved and error rates for 30 days, then expand what works.

A realistic first project

A good first project can be scoped, built and measured in weeks, not quarters. Examples include an intake assistant that turns inquiries into structured CRM records, a drafting workflow for proposals, or an automated weekly KPI summary. Win once, document it, and the next opportunity becomes obvious.
