Forensic bookkeeping: what your numbers may be hiding

By Michael FurthOctober 20266 min read

Most businesses don’t lose money in one dramatic event. They lose it quietly, a few hundred dollars at a time, in places nobody is looking. Forensic bookkeeping is the discipline of looking there on purpose.

What forensic bookkeeping is, and isn’t

Ordinary bookkeeping records what happened. Forensic bookkeeping asks whether what was recorded is complete, accurate and explainable. It rebuilds records where they’re missing, reconciles accounts that don’t tie out, and traces individual transactions from source document to ledger to bank.

It is not an audit, and it isn’t a legal or tax opinion. Those belong to licensed CPAs and attorneys. The goal is to give you, and them, a clear and documented picture to act on.

Where money usually leaks

  • Unbilled or under-billed work. Change orders, extra hours and reimbursable expenses that never made it onto an invoice.
  • Duplicate and erroneous payments. The same vendor invoice paid twice, or paid at the wrong rate.
  • Unapplied credits and deposits. Customer payments, vendor credits and refunds sitting unmatched.
  • Vendor creep. Price increases, fees and subscriptions that renewed quietly and were never challenged.
  • Misclassified costs. Expenses coded to the wrong account, distorting margins and hiding where profit really comes from.
  • Aging receivables. Old balances that nobody is collecting because nobody is sure they’re real.
The answer is usually hiding in plain sight. It’s just spread across three systems and two years.

The other side of the ledger: entitlements

Leakage is money going out that shouldn’t. Entitlements are money and advantages coming in that could. Many businesses qualify for things they’ve never claimed, such as tax credits, workforce training reimbursements, local incentives and small-business certifications that open doors to set-aside contracts. Finding them takes the same patience as finding leaks: reading the rules carefully, then matching them to your facts. Claims are then prepared and filed with your CPA.

How a forensic review works

  1. Scope the question. Are you after a specific discrepancy, general leakage, or clean records before a sale, loan or audit?
  2. Gather the sources. Bank and card statements, invoices, contracts, payroll and the accounting file itself.
  3. Rebuild and reconcile. Tie every account out, document every adjustment and flag every exception.
  4. Trace and quantify. Follow the exceptions to their cause and put a dollar value on each.
  5. Report and fix. Rank findings by impact, recover what’s recoverable, and put controls in place so it doesn’t recur.

When to call for one

If the bank balance and the books disagree, if margins feel lower than the work suggests, if you’re preparing for a transaction, or if a bookkeeper has left and nobody is quite sure what state things are in, a forensic review usually pays for itself, and brings peace of mind.
