Forensic bookkeeping: where the money leaks

By Michael FurthOctober 20264 min read

Most businesses don’t lose money in one event. They lose it a few hundred dollars at a time in accounts nobody reconciles and invoices nobody matches. Forensic bookkeeping means looking in those places on purpose.

What it is, and what it isn’t

Ordinary bookkeeping records what happened. Forensic bookkeeping asks whether the record is complete and explainable: whether every account ties out to the bank, and whether every payment traces back to a document. Where records are missing, I rebuild them from bank and card statements, then trace transactions from source document to ledger to bank.

It is not an audit, and it doesn’t produce a tax or legal opinion. Those belong to licensed CPAs and attorneys. My job is documented findings that you and they can rely on.

Where money usually leaks

  • Bank reconciliations that only look finished. A reconciliation that balances because of a plug entry, or that carries checks outstanding for a year, hasn’t been reconciled.
  • Duplicate payments. The same invoice paid twice, entered once as “INV-1043” and once as “1043”, or paid to a vendor set up twice. I sort payments by vendor and amount and look for near-matches.
  • Paying without a three-way match. When nobody compares the purchase order, the receiving ticket and the invoice, you pay for short shipments and for prices nobody agreed to.
  • Unapplied cash. Customer payments recorded but never applied to an invoice. AR aging then overstates what’s owed, and good customers get collection calls for bills they’ve paid.
  • Retainage nobody bills. In construction, retainage receivable waits for closeout, and if nobody tracks the release conditions it never gets invoiced.
  • Unbilled change orders. Extra work approved in the field that never made it onto an invoice.
  • Misclassified costs. Expenses coded to the wrong job distort margins and hide which work makes money.
The answer is usually in plain sight. It’s spread across three systems and two years of statements.

The other side of the ledger: entitlements

Leakage is money going out that shouldn’t. Entitlements are money coming in that could, found the same way: read the rule, then match it to your facts.

The R&D credit can reach more ordinary work than owners expect, such as developing a new product or process, but the claim rests on documenting the work and its costs, ideally as it happens. The Work Opportunity Tax Credit depends on screening paperwork completed at hire, within a short deadline, so first check whether your onboarding includes it. Certifications such as HUBZone or DBE can also open set-aside contracts. I find and document these; the claims are prepared and filed with your CPA, who decides what the business is entitled to.

How a review works

  1. Scope the question. One discrepancy is a different job from cleaning up the books before a sale. The question sets how far back to go.
  2. Gather the sources. Bank and card statements first, because they’re the one record nobody at the company typed. Invoices and contracts come next; payroll and the accounting file last.
  3. Rebuild and reconcile. Tie every account to its statement. Each adjustment gets a note, and each exception goes on a list.
  4. Trace and quantify. Follow every exception to its cause and put a dollar figure on it.
  5. Report and fix. Rank findings by dollar impact and recover what you can. Then add the control that stops a repeat.

When to call for one, and when not to

Call for one if the bank balance and the books disagree, if margins look thin for the work, if you’re heading into a sale or a loan, or if a bookkeeper has left and nobody knows the state of the file.

Skip it if your books are small and reconciled monthly by someone you trust; a review would mostly confirm what you know. If you suspect theft rather than error, your first call goes to your attorney. Leave the records untouched; evidence is handled differently from then on.
